# Results

Generated by `python3 -m bqbench results-doc` — do not edit by hand.

Medians over n = 6–18 interleaved repetitions, result cache off, on-demand
pricing, US multi-region. `ratio` is against the first variant.

**Three meters, and they disagree.** `slot-ms` is aggregate slot consumption
across parallel workers — what capacity pricing bills. `elapsed-ms` is wall
clock — what a human waits. `billed` is bytes — what on-demand pricing charges,
and the only one of the three that is money here. A rewrite can move one and
not the others, so each ratio is reported against its own meter and none of
them is called "faster" or "cheaper" without saying which. `p` and `95% CI`
describe the slot-ms column only.

**Read the CI, not the p-value.** `p` is a two-sided Mann-Whitney U and is
indicative only; `95% CI` is a percentile bootstrap on the median ratio, and it
is the column that says what a comparison actually establishes. A CI spanning
1.00 means no difference was detected — *and* bounds how large one could have
gone undetected. At these sample sizes that bound is often wide, so "no
difference" frequently means "nothing bigger than ±20%", not "identical".

Where a comparison shows identical plans, identical record counts and identical
billed bytes, that structural evidence is far stronger than the timing, and the
CI merely bounds any residual. See
[METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md#multiple-comparisons).

Raw per-job records with complete query plans are published as a release asset;
see the README.

## Myth 1b follow-up — replace the regex instead of reordering it

Three `LOWER() LIKE` clauses against the single regex they replace. The obvious fix is worse than the thing it fixes. Note these two are NOT semantically identical: the regex anchors on word boundaries and the LIKE clauses match substrings, so they return different row sets. ## Myth 2 — INNER beats LEFT beats OUTER

## Myth 4 — referenced three times

The case the folklore is actually about. CTE and subquery do the same work stage for stage and bill the same 348 MiB - the base table is scanned twice and the 5.2M-row aggregate read back twice, which is what the 74,257,782 stage-sum is made of. The temp table is worse on every meter, and the cost is the write, not the read-back: its `CREATE TEMP TABLE` child job burns 51,918 slot-ms while all three references together burn 268. Temp-table stages are n/a: a script's work lives in child jobs, which carry no parent-level plan, and the `records read` of 0 is that absence, not a measurement.

> Avoid CTEs, use temp tables - the multiply-referenced case.

## Myth 7 follow-up — casting when NEITHER side is INT64

The case anyone with an unmigrated schema actually has, and it settles what m7 could not. Casting both sides removes 73% of the slot-time penalty (1.38x -> 1.10x) and NONE of the byte penalty: both STRING variants bill 504 MiB against native INT64's 458. A CAST cannot shrink what you scan. So on on-demand pricing, where bytes are the bill, casting at query time buys you nothing at all - migrating the column is the only thing that does.

## Denormalisation break-even

The measured cost of rebuilding the wide table against the measured per-query
saving — the arithmetic the advice never includes.

```
per-query saving         47,632 slot-ms       156.0 MiB billed
one rebuild costs       425,009 slot-ms      2179.0 MiB billed   (median of 5)

break-even, slot time     8.9 queries per rebuild
break-even, on-demand    14.0 queries per rebuild

wide table           2.152 GiB
3 normalised         2.127 GiB   -> the duplication is +1.2%
wide table storage $  0.043/month   (additive: the rebuild reads the 3, so they stay)
nightly rebuild    $  0.390/month   (9x that storage)
```

The duplication everyone warns about is 1.2% — the user columns repeat across
posts, but a narrow row repeated is still a narrow row. That is the answer to
"won't denormalising blow up my storage", and it is no.

It is also the wrong question. You cannot drop the three source tables, because
the rebuild joins them, so the wide table's full 2.152 GiB is added on top —
and even that is not what binds. The refresh is: below roughly 9–14 queries
per rebuild cycle you spend more refreshing the table than you save reading it.
